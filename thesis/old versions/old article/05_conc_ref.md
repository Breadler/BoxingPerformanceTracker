V.	DISCUSSION AND FUTURE WORK
A.	Interpretation
The evaluation results in Section IV-E support the reliability of the manual labelling and feature-generation pipeline. All 175 labelled punch windows referenced a valid pose video, and duration statistics were tightly clustered around a 240 ms median, suggesting that manual frame-level punch boundaries were applied consistently despite being produced by a single reviewer working across 15 different videos. The zero-defect audit result (no missing video IDs, invalid frame ranges, or out-of-range labels) indicates that the labelling and auditing workflow, rather than any single manual pass, is what should be trusted to catch annotation errors before they propagate into the training dataset.
The low usable-video yield (15 of 49 reviewed, or 31%) is, on reflection, a result in its own right rather than a limitation of the labelling algorithm: it quantifies how much of an uncurated dataset like UCF101 survives MediaPipe's practical tracking requirements once camera framing, occlusion, and multi-person shots are accounted for. Framing this yield rate explicitly gives future work a concrete baseline to improve on by sourcing cleaner footage.
B.	Limitations
Three limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. 
First, all labelling was performed by a single annotator; while the audit and duration-consistency checks provide indirect evidence of internal consistency, this evaluation cannot report inter-rater reliability, since no second annotator's labels exist for comparison.
Second, the training videos are drawn from the UCF101 public dataset rather than self-recorded footage collected under controlled conditions; this substitution was made for convenience and scale, but it means variation in camera angle, lighting, background, and frame rate is inherent to the dataset, and was the direct cause of the low usable-video yield reported in Section IV-E.
Third, due to time constraints, punch type (such as straight, hook, uppercut) was not labelled, so while the resulting dataset is suitable for punch detection, it cannot yet support the richer, technique-level analysis that motivated the broader project.
C.	Future Work
Building on the labelled dataset produced here, the most immediate step is the continued development of the broader boxing performance tracker application will integrate the trained Random Forest classifier described in Section IV-E into a working mobile prototype. Second, labelling additional UCF101 videos beyond the 15 used here would directly improve dataset size and diversity, and partially offset the yield limitation noted above. Third, the labelling schema itself could be extended, either by distinguishing specific punch types such as straight punches, hooks, and uppercuts, or by adding hip rotation as an additional tracked and computed feature, which would let the dataset capture a biomechanically important component of punching technique that the current wrist- and elbow-centred feature set does not directly represent.
