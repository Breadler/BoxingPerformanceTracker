Labelling and Feature-Engineering Algorithm for Pose-Based Punch Detection of Boxing Video




 
 
 
 
 
Abstract— Training a Random Forest classifier to detect punches and fatigue-related form degradation from smartphone boxing video requires a labelled ground-truth dataset linking pose landmarks to punch events. This paper presents and evaluates a manual labelling pipeline for this purpose. Boxing clips drawn from the UCF101 dataset are processed with MediaPipe Pose Landmarker to extract per-frame 3D body landmarks; a human reviewer then watches an annotated, skeleton-overlaid review video to mark the start and end frame of each punch. These manual labels are converted into fixed-duration, millisecond-based training windows, end-anchored to each punch's impact frame, and combined with motion- and body-relative features, including wrist and elbow velocity, and position relative to the shoulder and body centre, to build a supervised training set. Frame-based labels are automatically converted to milliseconds so that windows represent consistent real-world durations despite the variable frame rates present in UCF101 footage. Pose landmarks were extracted for 163 videos; of 49 videos manually reviewed, 15 (31%) yielded usable footage, producing 175 labelled punch windows with a mean duration of 210.7 ms. An automated audit confirmed zero label-integrity errors. These results support the reliability of the labelling pipeline as a foundation for training a Random Forest punch classifier and for future extensions such as punch-type labelling.
Keywords— pose estimation, boxing punch, video annotation, landmark labelling, Random Forest, MediaPipe, action recognition 
I.	INTRODUCTION
Sports training has traditionally relied on manual observation by coaches or athletes, which can be inconsistent, time-consuming, and prone to human error [1]. In striking sports such as boxing, maintaining proper form is important for both performance and injury prevention. However, as fatigue sets in during training, athletes often experience form degradation without realising it. With recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) and deep learning, pose estimation models now make it possible to analyse human movement directly from standard video input, eliminating the need for motion-capture suits or specialised hardware. Although research has demonstrated the potential integration of AI-based pose estimation into sports environments, there are currently few mobile solutions that provide real-time, camera-only performance tracking tailored to individual athletes.
The broader project this paper supports is a boxing punch-detection pipeline built around video pose estimation and a Random Forest classifier. Pose landmarks are extracted from boxing videos, punch moments are manually labelled by frame number, the pose data and labels are converted into a training dataset, a Random Forest model is trained to distinguish punch from no punch windows, and the trained model is then applied to new user footage to estimate punch count and timing. This full pipeline has been implemented end to end: pose extraction, dataset construction, classifier training, and inference on new footage.
This paper focuses specifically on the labelling stage that makes the rest of the pipeline possible. Video is first processed automatically through MediaPipe Pose Landmarker to extract per-frame body landmarks. A manual labelling system is used: annotated review videos, with the pose skeleton and frame number drawn on top, are reviewed by hand, and punch start and end frames are recorded. These manual labels are then combined with the per-frame pose data by a labelling algorithm that generates fixed-size training windows and computes motion-relative and body-relative features, such as wrist velocity and wrist position relative to the shoulder, needed to tell a punch apart from ordinary movement. An automated auditing step further checks the manual labels for consistency before they are used. The reliability of this manual labelling and feature-generation pipeline is then evaluated against independent manual review.

II.	LITERATURE REVIEW
Recent advancements in AI, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have enabled markerless pose estimation directly from standard RGB video, unlocking an accessible way to track movement, technique, and physical performance without specialised hardware. Within striking sports such as boxing, pose estimation research is growing but still relatively limited, with much of the existing work focused on isolated aspects of performance, such as punch counting for judging, rather than a time-based analysis of technical degradation or fatigue across rounds.
A.	Pose Estimation and Random Forest Studies
Souaifi et al. [2] conducted a broad scoping review of AI applications across sports biomechanics, confirming the overall shift toward mobile, markerless, real-time solutions, though without experimental validation of any single method. Patil et al. [3] demonstrated a functional camera-to-feedback pipeline for medical rehabilitation exercises, showing that pose estimation, rule-based scoring, and dashboard visualisation can run outside laboratory settings; however, their approach targets slow, controlled movement and is not challenged by the occlusion or high-speed dynamics seen in boxing. Bulun and Berg [4] developed a real-time mobile prototype using BlazePose and a Random Forest classifier to detect jabs and guard drops, which directly validates the feasibility of smartphone-based boxing analysis. However, their system is narrow in scope and does not document how their training labels were produced. Magalhães et al. [5] confirmed that markerless motion capture can match marker-based accuracy for boxing upper-body kinematics, though under professional multi-camera conditions, while Lin et al. (2023) showed that monocular RGB video contains sufficient implicit depth information for 3D pose reconstruction, at the cost of computational complexity unsuitable for mobile deployment [6].
Separately, Random Forest classifiers have proven effective at mapping biomechanical or kinematic features to movement states: Park et al. [7] introduced a verification-forest stage that filters noisy per-frame pose votes to improve robustness to occlusion; Prodanović et al. [8] applied Random Forest to detect abnormal knee-joint patterns and explicitly discussed preprocessing, feature selection, and labelling strategy, though their ground truth came from clinical motion-capture data rather than manually reviewed consumer video; and Ahmadi et al. [9] classified whole time windows of wearable-sensor data (rather than individual frames), achieving close to 98% accuracy while reducing the granularity of manual annotation required — a windowed-labelling approach conceptually similar to the manual, window-based punch labelling adopted in this project, though derived from IMU sensors instead of visual landmarks.
B.	Comparative Analysis of Existing Applications
Jabbr functions as an automated “AI boxing judge,” producing 2D punch statistics from footage but without full-body posture tracking or time-series visualisation of form across rounds. Growl combines wearable inertial sensors with a smart punching pad to deliver precise force, speed, and volume telemetry, but requires proprietary hardware that limits accessibility for casual athletes. Tempo offers AI-driven 3D-sensing home-gym coaching with real-time corrective feedback, but is tailored to general strength-training movements and depends on dedicated hardware and a subscription model. None of these systems trains a classifier on manually labelled video landmarks, and consequently none document or require a labelling pipeline of the kind proposed in this paper.
C.	Summary
The literature confirms that markerless pose estimation from smartphone video is accurate and fast enough to support boxing-specific analysis, and that Random Forest classifiers are well suited to mapping landmark-derived features to movement and fatigue states. However, none of the reviewed boxing-specific or Random Forest studies document or validate the manual labelling process used to produce their training data, and none combine manually reviewed punch windows with an algorithm that derives frame-level labels and additional biomechanical metrics from raw landmark coordinates. This paper addresses that gap directly.
